Economics is complicated. The only thing certain across its disciplines is that claims of *certainty* about what will happen are certainly wrong. What economics can do is work in *approximations*. It's not so much an engine for prediction as a backward-looking summary of observational data, and from there we can get rough ideas of what could or should happen if those circumstances arise again. That can, understandably, be highly confusing for anyone who hasn't spent significant time learning not just what they're looking at in a chart, but what the takeaway is even meant to be. I found differential calculus incredibly hard. For some, statistics is their biggest academic villain.

Part of what drew me to the subject as an undergrad was an odd split among my peers. Some described the field as a lens that would change how I saw the modern world. Others called it guesstimation and witchcraft, with no practical purpose for the average person carefully investing for retirement. Either way, both camps had one thing in common: they asked every econ grad they could find the same questions. What's going on with the economy? What do you think of (insert current event)? Does the debt even matter?

Conversation can only answer those so well, time and time again. I'm a visual learner, so I built this interactive article to share my broadest two cents on the US economy, in four points: what's gotten unaffordable, who owns the wealth, what the debt really risks, and why the US is still the richest nation on earth. These are the issues I see as relevant to our time, observable in the data, and of concern to anyone at any level of participation in our civic system.
