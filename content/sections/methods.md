Every number on this page is computed from the public datasets listed below by a script that downloads them, so the charts and text update together when the data is refreshed. The last refresh was {{fact:refresh_date}}.

- **Price indexes (Charts 1a and 1b).** Each series is divided by its own average over the base year (2000, or 1985 in the longer view) and multiplied by 100, so every line starts at 100 and 250 means "2.5 times the base-year price". The lines use seasonally adjusted data; the bars compare the latest 12 months ({{fact:prices_period}}) with the base-year average, using data that isn't seasonally adjusted. Wages are median usual weekly earnings of full-time wage and salary workers, before taxes. CPI measures the price of the same quality of item over time.
- **State affordability (Map 1).** From the Census Bureau's American Community Survey 5-year estimates ({{fact:acs_vintage}}): five years of survey responses pooled into one estimate, so even small states have a reliable figure, at the cost of being less current than a single year.
- **Wealth (Chart 2a).** Federal Reserve Distributional Financial Accounts: total household net worth split by wealth group. Social Security isn't counted as wealth.
- **Debt and spending (Section 3).** History from OMB's Historical Tables; the projection is CBO's baseline ({{fact:cbo_vintage}}), which assumes current law stays as written. It's a projection, not a forecast.
- **Countries (Section 4).** GDP per person is at purchasing power parity (PPP): adjusted for what money buys locally, so countries are compared by living standards, not exchange rates. Countries are ranked in the latest year most of them report. The interest comparison uses total GDP at market exchange rates. Extreme poverty uses the World Bank's {{fact:poverty_line}} line; relative poverty is the OECD's share of people living on less than half their country's median income, after taxes and transfers.
- **Number line.** The five amounts are round numbers for scale, not data.
