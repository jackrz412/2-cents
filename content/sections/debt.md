The national debt matters, but we can't think of it like a traditional loan. The US government borrows in a currency it issues itself. That would be akin to you promising someone $40,000 in five years, then materializing the money out of thin air when the bill comes due. There are absolutely levers we can pull to pay our debt obligations. Debt held by the public is {{fact:debt_gdp_latest}}% of GDP (FY{{fact:debt_gdp_latest_year}}), and the Congressional Budget Office projects it will pass its post-World War II record of {{fact:debt_gdp_peak}}% in {{fact:cbo_passes_peak_year}}.

A far more pressing issue is credit, and how it's perceived. The US can keep borrowing so long as people believe in its ability to pay them back, which is often called creditworthiness. Lenders at home and abroad trust this so much that they buy Treasury bonds as "risk-free" assets in their own wealth strategies; foreign investors hold {{fact:foreign_share}}% of the debt held by the public. The price of that trust is the interest rate. In {{fact:yield_10y_period}}, the 10-year Treasury yield was {{fact:yield_10y}}%.

If we were to default, whether by political blunder or by real unaffordability (revenue problems, ballooning interest), that trust could be destroyed. Investors would no longer want our bonds, which would shrink how much we can borrow and how much of our government we can fund, and raise the cost of borrowing. And if we printed money to service the debt, we'd risk inflation beyond what our system expects and tolerates. Germany after World War I is the textbook case.

Interest is already a big line item. Defense is often pointed to as one of our largest expenditures, and a frequent target for people who'd rather see that money go to social programs and the public interest. Since FY{{fact:interest_above_defense_since}}, we've spent more on interest than on the entire military: ${{fact:net_interest_bn}} billion against ${{fact:defense_bn}} billion in FY{{fact:omb_latest_year}}. In other words, a year of interest is more than the entire economy of {{fact:interest_beats_country}}, ranked {{fact:interest_beats_rank}} in the world by GDP. Only {{fact:countries_bigger_than_interest}} countries have an economy bigger than our interest bill.

While scary, it's important to remember that this debt is backed by the largest Western economy and the most valuable business sector on Earth. We're on a mathematical trajectory toward debt problems, but we still have time to make informed adjustments to how our government borrows and spends, and bring this to a manageable level.
