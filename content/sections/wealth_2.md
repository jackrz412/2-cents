Our wealth distribution is a staggering display of magnitude. Most people hold extremely little of the total, and a tiny few hold most of the American pie: as of {{fact:wealth_period}}, the top 10% of households own {{fact:top10_share}}% of all household wealth, and the top 1% alone own {{fact:top1_share}}%. The bottom half own {{fact:bottom50_share}}%. It's also moving in one direction: in {{fact:wealth_first_period}}, the top 1% held {{fact:top1_share_first}}% and the bottom half {{fact:bottom50_share_first}}%. Income figures understate this. Income is what you earn in a year; wealth is what you've accumulated, and it compounds.

Two things soften the picture, without changing it. Part of the bottom half is simply young: most people start adult life with little or even negative net worth and build it over decades, so some of this concentration is life stage, not class. And these figures don't count Social Security, the largest retirement asset for most middle- and lower-income households. Counting it would make the distribution look less concentrated, though still very unequal.
