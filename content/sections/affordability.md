Not everything has become unaffordable for the average household. But some of the most essential foundations of our way of life (housing, medicine, education) have. College tuition in particular has departed remarkably from our growth in wages: since 2000, tuition is up {{fact:tuition_pct_since_2000}}% while median weekly earnings are up {{fact:wages_pct_since_2000}}%. Go back to 1985 and it's {{fact:tuition_pct_since_1985}}% against {{fact:wages_pct_since_1985}}%.

That gap, between how much more we earn and how much more (or less) things cost, is what people feel today. One sector outpacing wages would show up as a single line on a household budget. Several at once has an additive effect: workers and households feel a much smaller margin between earnings and expenses than in years gone by, or than previous generations had. Overall prices actually rose a little *slower* than wages ({{fact:cpi_pct_since_1985}}% since 1985), so the squeeze isn't everything getting pricier. It's the essentials, the things you can't skip. You can put off a new TV; you can't put off rent or a hospital bill.

The counterpoint is worth naming. Sectors synonymous with our tech-driven economy have gotten far cheaper than just a few decades ago. TV prices fell {{fact:tv_pct_since_1985|abs}}% since 1985, and computers {{fact:computers_pct_since_2000|abs}}% since 2000, making them more affordable now than at any point in the data, and of much, much higher quality and complexity. That quality is part of the number: price indexes track the cost of the *same* quality of item over time, so much of that drop means you get far more TV for the money.
